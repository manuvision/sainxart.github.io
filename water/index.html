Behind the numbers

I’m from the Caribbean. When I read about the water AI uses, I think about the places where getting water is already a problem.

Why I made this

In parts of Guadeloupe, there is a weekly timetable for when the water gets cut off. The water supplier calls these rotating cuts tours d’eau. A basic part of everyday life has to fit around a schedule.

SMGEAG: water-cut schedule, September 7–13, 2026 ↗

Access is only part of it. Chlordecone, a pesticide used on banana plantations in Guadeloupe and Martinique until 1993, still contaminates affected soils, rivers, and coastal waters decades later.

Assurance Maladie: the lasting pollution from chlordecone ↗

Then there is sargassum. Mass strandings have been recurring along French Caribbean shores since 2011. As the seaweed decomposes, it releases gases that can harm the people living nearby.

BRGM: sargassum and its environmental effects ↗

These are different problems, with different causes. They are the context I bring to this project; they are not caused by AI or included in this counter.

I use AI. I used it to help build this, too. That is part of the irony. I wanted to put the water it uses on screen, in a bottle familiar enough to make me stop and look. The research and the limits of the estimate are here so you can look closer.

What am I looking at?

The big number estimates how much water AI has used worldwide since midnight UTC. It updates from your device’s clock. It is an estimate, not a live reading from water meters.

The full bottle stands for one day’s estimated total: —. Its water volume matches the share used so far. At 20%, the bottle contains one-fifth of its capacity, including when you tilt it. Height and volume are different because the bottle has a narrow neck.

Why 51% at 8:14? At 08:14 in Toronto in September, it is 12:14 UTC: just over halfway through the UTC day. Everyone sees the same global clock.

The bottle is a daily scale, not a water supply, a safe limit, or a target. It fills toward 100%, then starts the next UTC day empty. It never overflows. The reset does not undo the water use. The historical total below keeps increasing.

Drag left or right to turn it; drag up or down to tilt it. Keyboard: arrow keys to turn, R to reset. Moving the bottle changes the waves, never the amount of water counted.

And since AI took off?

—estimated liters since January 1, 2012

This historical number is much less certain. There is no agreed date for the “first AI data center.” We use 2012 as a starting point for the modern deep-learning era associated with AlexNet.

To work backward from 2025, we assume water use had grown by 50% each year. That growth rate is our illustrative assumption, not a measured history. It excludes AI computing before 2012.

Using 25%, 50%, or 100% yearly growth instead would give roughly 2.57, 1.61, or 1.08 trillion liters through the end of 2025 at the middle estimate. The lower and higher buttons change the annual estimate only; they do not cover every uncertainty in history.

What counts as “water used”?

We count water consumed by data-center cooling and by the electricity supporting AI. This often means water evaporates rather than returning immediately to the same local water supply. It does not mean water vanishes from the planet.

Making chips and building facilities are excluded. Water withdrawal is a different measure: it also includes water that is taken in and then returned. We do not mix those larger figures into this counter.

How the clock becomes liters

Today’s liters = yearly estimate × seconds since midnight ÷ seconds in the year

After 2025, we keep the yearly estimate constant. This makes the counter a simple reference scenario, not a prediction of actual water use in 2026 or later. Each UTC day resets at midnight, and leap years use their actual length.

The IEA’s April 2026 outlook projects that AI-focused data centers’ electricity use will roughly triple from 2025 to 2030. Water use does not necessarily grow at the same rate, so we do not automatically apply that increase here.

How the water moves

The bottle uses a lightweight surface-wave simulation with gravity, inertia, and damping. Waves respond to your movements and settle afterward. A volume solver adjusts the surface to keep the modeled amount of water constant as the bottle tilts and the waves move. Tiny idle ripples keep it alive.

This is an interactive approximation, not a full three-dimensional engineering fluid simulation. Ripples, reflections, and the bottle itself illustrate the data; they are not measurements. The volume calculation uses the bottle’s actual inner shape and numerical sampling. Pause stops the waves; the clock and water amount keep updating. Reduced-motion preferences are respected.
