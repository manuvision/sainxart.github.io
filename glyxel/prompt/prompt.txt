MASTER PROMPT — PIXEL SPRITE → DUAL-ELEMENT 10-MINUTE COPIC CREATURE ENTRY

Use the supplied pixel-art image as the primary structural source and absolute color source.

Transform it into one finished hand-drawn creature-design page that feels like a personal Pokédex entry, creature journal, animation doodle, or quick traditional marker illustration.

The pixel sprite provides:

* silhouette evidence
* proportion
* mass relationships
* negative space
* the exact three-color palette

It does not provide a literal surface pattern that must be copied square-for-square.

Resolve the sprite into a creature with:

* coherent anatomy
* logical locomotion or suspension
* harmonized color placement
* three distinct material/texture languages
* two palette-derived elemental types
* one Bridge Color
* simple species-specific personality
* a traditional Copic + pencil + pen rendering style

The creature should remain simple enough that a beginner could plausibly reproduce it in under 10 minutes.

The hierarchy is:

pixel silhouette → coherent body plan → exact source palette → harmonized color anatomy → three material identities → dual elemental ecology → quick traditional illustration

The guiding principle is:

maximum character from minimum marks.

⸻

1. READ THE PIXEL SPRITE AS EVIDENCE

Extract:

* dominant silhouette
* overall width and height
* upper, central, lateral, and lower masses
* symmetry or asymmetry
* implied body center
* major negative spaces
* appendage placement
* implied balance
* approximate weight distribution
* relative scale of individual masses
* the exact three dominant non-black colors present in the source

Ask:

What simple creature, organism, construct, or hybrid could naturally produce this overall silhouette?

The finished creature should clearly descend from the sprite when compared side by side.

⸻

2. BLACK PIXELS ARE TRANSPARENT

Treat every black or near-black source pixel as transparent background / empty space.

Black does not contribute to:

* anatomy
* facial features
* markings
* pigmentation
* texture selection
* elemental type
* body material

Use black only in the finished traditional illustration for:

* outlines
* sparse hatching
* small shadow accents
* handwritten text

The creature palette consists of exactly three non-black source colors.

⸻

3. SOURCE COLOR FIDELITY IS NON-NEGOTIABLE

The three non-black colors in the source pixel art are the authoritative palette for the final creature.

Preserve their actual visual identities as closely as possible.

Do not merely approximate them as generic:

* purple
* peach
* blue
* green
* orange

Instead preserve:

* exact hue family
* warm/cool bias
* saturation
* lightness
* relative contrast
* relative dominance

The finished creature must look as though it was colored using the same three colors that appear in the pixel sprite.

If the matched Copic marker name is slightly different from the visible source color, the source pixel color wins visually.

The Copic label functions as a physical marker reference, not permission to shift the creature away from the source palette.

⸻

4. COPIC MATCHING MUST FOLLOW THE INPUT

When matching each source color to the fixed 12-marker Copic system:

1. sample or visually infer the actual source color
2. select the closest permitted marker
3. preserve the source color appearance in the creature
4. use the marker code/name only as the nearest physical reference

Do not exaggerate the marker’s canonical color if that causes drift from the source.

For example:

If a source violet is muted and dusty, do not render it as a much brighter saturated violet simply because its nearest marker is V09.

The creature should match the sprite first.

The swatch label should identify the nearest available marker second.

⸻

5. SILHOUETTE FIDELITY WITHOUT PIXEL LITERALISM

Preserve the sprite’s strongest relationships:

* overall top width
* body width
* central alignment
* side-mass placement
* lower-form spacing
* major open gaps
* stance width
* compact, tall, broad, narrow, or top-heavy character
* symmetry when visually important

The final anatomy must also make sense according to its own physical logic.

The creature does not necessarily need to be:

* humanoid
* quadrupedal
* bipedal
* vertebrate
* fully organic

It may be:

* floating
* serpentine
* limbless
* tripod
* multi-legged
* shell-based
* fungal
* botanical
* aquatic
* coral-like or mineral
* mechanical
* biomechanical
* synthetic-organic
* suspended
* hovering
* colony-like
* asymmetrical

Choose whatever body plan best explains the source silhouette.

⸻

6. THE PIXEL GRID IS NOT THE CREATURE’S SKIN

Pixel positions describe broad structural evidence.

They do not prescribe literal markings.

Do not translate alternating pixel colors into:

* checkerboards
* square spots
* rectangular stripes
* chessboard patterns
* stacked cubes
* floating square plates
* repeated block markings
* pixel-shaped armor
* tiled skin

Instead, redistribute the colors according to the final anatomy.

Preserve the exact three source color identities and their approximate visual importance, not their square-by-square coordinates.

⸻

7. HARMONIZE COLOR PLACEMENT

Use the three source colors as 2–3 major harmonious anatomical color regions across the whole creature.

Color placement should follow:

* body planes
* dorsal vs. ventral surfaces
* appendage structure
* material boundaries
* armor divisions
* membrane edges
* belly regions
* fur masses
* shell interiors
* joints
* elemental adaptations
* sensory regions

Favor:

* rounded areas
* tapered transitions
* broad patches
* curved boundaries
* natural irregularity
* clear material changes

The creature should look intentionally color-designed even if the viewer never saw the sprite.

The exact colors themselves must still remain faithful to the input.

⸻

8. COLOR HIERARCHY

Of the three source colors:

* one becomes the Bridge Color
* one primarily represents Element A
* one primarily represents Element B

A useful starting proportion is:

* 50–60% Bridge Color
* 20–30% Element A color
* 15–25% Element B color

Adjust when the silhouette or source color balance suggests another proportion.

Preserve the relative visual weight of the source colors where possible.

Use broad, calm color fields.

⸻

9. LONELY PIXEL RULE

A standalone colored pixel or tiny cluster of 2–3 pixels is a suggestion, not a literal object.

Do not automatically turn isolated pixels into:

* floating squares
* tiny cubes
* checker fragments
* detached rectangular plates
* arbitrary hovering blocks

Instead ask what broader design idea those pixels might imply.

Possible interpretations include:

* tip of an appendage
* edge of a membrane
* small protrusion
* joint
* sensory structure
* tail end
* toe or pad
* antenna
* crest
* small armor extension
* part of a larger continuous color region

You may:

* merge the isolated pixels into a nearby anatomical mass
* attach them with a thin organic or mechanical connection
* enlarge them into one coherent rounded form
* reinterpret them as part of an existing limb
* adapt surrounding anatomy so they become structurally logical
* redistribute their color elsewhere while preserving overall palette balance

The priority is always:

coherent creature design over literal pixel matching.

⸻

10. DETACHED FORMS MUST MAKE SENSE

If the silhouette strongly suggests a separated or floating mass, give it a visually understandable relationship to the main body.

It may be connected through:

* membrane
* filament
* stalk
* tendon
* root
* fur bridge
* cable

* flexible joint
* transparent tissue
* mechanical rail
* magnetic suspension
* psychic suspension
* buoyancy
* elemental lift
* orbit around a central structure

If no convincing reason exists for the part to remain detached, attach, merge, enlarge, simplify, or reinterpret it.

Never preserve a floating form merely because several pixels appeared separated in the sprite.

⸻

11. COHERENT BODY PLAN

Before adding details, establish:

* where the creature’s center of mass is
* how major parts connect
* how it moves
* how it supports itself
* what direction is forward
* which feature is its main silhouette idea

Possible movement systems include:

* walking
* hopping
* crawling
* swimming
* gliding
* burrowing
* rolling
* slithering
* hovering
* floating
* rooting
* climbing
* rotating

* mechanical propulsion

The silhouette should feel believable for its chosen locomotion system.

⸻

12. REDUCE THE CREATURE TO 3–5 MAJOR FORMS

Use approximately 3–5 major shapes total.

Secondary features should be extremely limited.

Complexity should come from design ideas and material contrast, not from the number of anatomical pieces.

The creature should remain recognizable from a very simple filled silhouette.

⸻

13. 10-MINUTE DRAWABILITY

The finished creature should plausibly fit:

* 1–2 minutes — red-pencil construction
* 2 minutes — black line
* 3 minutes — Copic blocking
* 1–2 minutes — colored-pencil texture
* under 1 minute — white highlights and finishing accents

Therefore use:

* broad forms
* few interior lines
* limited hatching
* minimal micro-texture
* minimal environment
* no intricate anatomy

The final image should feel like an illustration/doodle, not a detailed concept-art painting.

⸻

14. MATERIAL + TEXTURE SYSTEM

Every creature should use at least three distinct material or texture identities.

At the beginning of each creature generation:

1. select three distinct textures/materials from the library below
2. assign exactly one primary material identity to each of the three source colors
3. keep those color-to-material relationships consistent throughout that creature
4. integrate them according to anatomy

The selection may vary between creatures, but the final combination must remain physically and visually coherent.

For example:

* source Color 1 → short fur
* source Color 2 → translucent membrane
* source Color 3 → matte shell

The material assignment must never change the actual source color itself.

Texture changes how the color is rendered or the black gel pen details added on top, not what hue it becomes.

16. THREE COLORS = THREE MATERIAL IDENTITIES

Assign one distinct material to each source color.

Example:

Source Color A → fur
Source Color B → ceramic armor
Source Color C → mucus-coated skin

Maintain that relationship consistently throughout the creature.

The actual hue must remain faithful to Source Color A, B, or C.

Do not alter the color because the selected material would conventionally be another hue.

For example:

* metal may still be violet
* fur may still be peach
* glass may still be green
* wood may still be blue

The creature follows the source palette, not real-world default material colors.

⸻

17. MATERIALS MUST FOLLOW ANATOMY

Assign materials to anatomically plausible regions.

For example:

* fur → torso, insulation zones, tail
* scales → protective outer surfaces
* mucus → exposed soft tissue
* feather → fins, wings, display structures
* metal → armor plates, structural joints
* glass → sensory dome or energy organ
* wood → limbs, roots, rigid botanical regions
* leather → flexible armored zones
* plastic → casing or biomechanical shell
* coral → growth surfaces
* membrane → thin side appendages

Use broad coherent zones rather than many tiny patches.

18. MATERIAL DIFFERENTIATION MUST REMAIN QUICK

Feather

1-3 broad feather divisions

Mucus

smooth marker + 1–2 white highlights

Wool

soft scalloped contour + minimal grain

Metal

flat marker plane + dark edge + white glint

Wood

1-3 pencil grain lines

Leather

matte marker + subtle edge shading

Glass

pale marker field + one or two white streaks

Plastic

flat clean marker area + one controlled highlight

Texture should read instantly without detailed rendering.

47. FINAL GENERATION PROCEDURE

STEP 1 — REMOVE BLACK

Treat black / near-black pixels as transparency.

STEP 2 — READ THE SILHOUETTE

Identify the strongest mass relationships and negative spaces.

STEP 3 — RESOLVE LONELY PIXELS

Treat tiny isolated clusters as flexible structural clues rather than literal floating squares.

STEP 4 — DISCOVER BODY PLAN

Choose coherent anatomy, locomotion, balance, and connectedness.

STEP 5 — REDUCE TO 3–5 FORMS

Simplify the entire creature.

STEP 6 — SAMPLE THE THREE SOURCE COLORS

Preserve their actual hue, value, saturation, and contrast.

STEP 7 — MATCH COPICS

Match them to exactly three markers from the fixed 12-color system while keeping the visible source colors authoritative.

STEP 8 — SELECT TWO TYPES

Choose the two strongest contrasting elemental identities.

STEP 9 — ASSIGN BRIDGE COLOR

Use the third as the visual unifier.

STEP 10 — RANDOMLY SELECT THREE MATERIALS

Choose three distinct textures/materials from the material library.

STEP 11 — ASSIGN ONE MATERIAL TO EACH COLOR

Keep each source-color/material pair consistent.

STEP 12 — HARMONIZE COLOR + MATERIAL PLACEMENT

Redistribute the exact source palette into 2–3 broad anatomical color regions.

STEP 13 — CREATE FACE/SENSORY FOCUS

Design one simple readable focal region.

STEP 14 — APPLY ELEMENT A

Add one major functional adaptation.

STEP 15 — APPLY ELEMENT B

Add one secondary functional adaptation.

STEP 16 — RED CONSTRUCTION

Add faint Col-Erase remnants.

STEP 17 — COPIC BLOCKING

Build the main shapes using colors that visually remain faithful to the source sprite.

STEP 18 — COLORED PENCIL

Add only enough texture to distinguish the materials, staying inside the same source color families.

STEP 19 — BLACK PEN

Strengthen silhouette, overlaps, sensory details, and a few shadows.

STEP 20 — WHITE GEL PEN

Add a handful of final material highlights.

STEP 21 — BUILD THE PAGE

Add name, tagline, sprite inset, three source-faithful swatches, labels, and two text-only types.

STEP 22 — CHECK PIXEL ARTIFACTS

Remove any checker, square, stripe, or floating-block interpretation from the creature itself.

STEP 23 — CHECK COLOR FIDELITY

Compare final creature colors directly against the source sprite and correct any visible drift.

STEP 24 — 10-MINUTE CHECK

Simplify again if the creature appears laborious to redraw.

48. ABSOLUTE FINAL INTENT

The final page should feel like:

a quick, inventive traditional creature doodle discovered from a three-color pixel silhouette, using the sprite’s actual palette rather than merely borrowing its general color family.

The pixel sprite determines:

silhouette, mass, proportion, negative space, and exact three-color palette.

Tiny pixel clusters are:

design clues, not literal detached blocks.

The three colors become:

2–3 broad, harmonized anatomical regions with three distinct material identities.

The selected Copic markers are:

physical reference labels for the actual source colors, not replacements for them.

The elemental pair determines:

two simple ecological or functional adaptations.

The rendering is built visibly from:

source-faithful Copic marker color → colored pencil texture → black pen structure → sparse white gel-pen highlights.
